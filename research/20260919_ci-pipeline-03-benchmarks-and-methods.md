# CI/CD, Code Review & Merge Workflow Benchmarks and Methodologies (2024-2026)

> **Verification key** used throughout: **[V]** = number pulled directly from a fetched primary/secondary source in this session (quoted or closely paraphrased, with URL). **[R]** = recalled from training knowledge, not confirmed against a live source this session — treat with more caution and re-verify before using in a decision doc. Every numeric claim below is tagged.

## Research Summary

Industry data through 2026 converges on one theme: **AI coding agents have removed the code-_generation_ bottleneck and moved it downstream to review, CI validation, and merge integration.** DORA's 2025 report [V], CircleCI's 2026 report [V], and LinearB's 2026 report [V] independently confirm the same shape — throughput (PRs opened, workflows run) is up sharply, but merge/deploy success rates, recovery time, and PR pickup time are all getting _worse_, not better, for the median team. A small number of "elite" teams pull away by investing specifically in fast feedback loops, small PRs, trunk-based development, and automated merge queues — the same practices DORA has recommended since 2018, now under new pressure from agent-authored volume.

## Part 1 — Benchmarks

### 1. DORA / State of DevOps (2023-2025)

- **2024 tiering (classic, still widely cited):** Elite performers deploy on demand (multiple times/day), lead time for changes under one hour, failed deployment recovery time under one hour, change failure rate 0-15%. Low performers take one to six months to ship a single change **[V]** ([Octopus Deploy, "2024 DevOps Performance Clusters"](https://octopus.com/blog/2024-devops-performance-clusters)).
- Elite vs. low performer _gaps_ cited in the 2024 cycle: elite teams deploy **182x more often**, have **8x lower change failure rates**, **127x faster** change lead times, and recover **2,293x faster** from failed deployments than low performers **[V]** ([Octopus Deploy](https://octopus.com/blog/2024-devops-performance-clusters), [Multitudes DORA guide](https://www.multitudes.com/blog/dora-metrics)). These multiplier figures are widely repeated across 2024/2025 secondary sources but I could not independently confirm the exact wording against the primary Google/DORA PDF this session — treat the multipliers as **[V]** for "reported," **[R]**-adjacent for "authoritative primary-source phrasing."
- **2024 introduced a 5th metric, Rework Rate** (deployment rework rate) — the percentage of deployments that are unplanned/fix-driven work, added because change failure rate alone was found to be a weak proxy for how much delivery capacity goes to fixing rather than shipping **[V]** ([CD Foundation, "The DORA 4 key metrics become 5," Oct 2025](https://cd.foundation/blog/2025/10/16/dora-5-metrics/)).
- **2025 DORA report — structural change:** replaced the old low/medium/high/elite four-tier ladder with **seven team archetypes** (e.g., "Foundational challenges" = low performance + high instability + high burnout; "Harmonious high achievers" = strong across delivery, wellbeing, and product outcomes), reflecting that AI-era teams no longer cluster cleanly on a single throughput/stability axis **[V]** ([DORA 2025 report landing](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/), summarized via Scrum.org and others). Survey base: **nearly 5,000 technology professionals** globally, plus **100+ hours** of qualitative interviews **[V]** ([Google Cloud, "Announcing the 2025 DORA report"](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)).
- **2025 DORA AI findings (the headline result):** **90%** of respondents report using AI at work; **80%+** believe it increased their individual productivity; but only **~70%** (i.e., ~30% report "little or no trust") trust AI-generated code **[V]** (same Google Cloud source). Critically, the report finds a **positive** relationship between AI adoption and _throughput_ (a reversal from the 2024 report, which found AI adoption correlated with _reduced_ throughput) but a persistent **negative** relationship between AI adoption and _delivery stability_ **[V]** ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report); [DORA "Balancing AI tensions"](https://dora.dev/insights/balancing-ai-tensions/)). DORA's own framing: **"AI doesn't fix a team; it amplifies what's already there"** — teams with weak automated testing, weak version control discipline, and slow feedback loops see instability rise faster than throughput gains justify **[V]**.
- I was not able to pull the literal 2025-report numeric table for deployment frequency / lead time / CFR / recovery time by archetype from a primary source in this session (the DORA site and Google Cloud blog pages I fetched are narrative, not tabular, in their crawlable form). Anyone citing exact 2025 archetype-level thresholds should pull the PDF directly from `dora.dev` — flagging this as a **gap**, not a fabricated number.

### 2. CircleCI State of Software Delivery (2024-2026)

- **2026 report** (most current; published ~Feb 2026, covers Sept 2025 data): **28,738,317 workflows** analyzed, from projects with ≥2 contributors and ≥5 runs **[V]** ([CircleCI, "Five takeaways from the 2026 report"](https://circleci.com/blog/five-takeaways-2026-software-delivery-report)).
  - **Main-branch success rate: 70.8%** — described as the lowest in over five years; CircleCI's stated benchmark target is **90%** **[V]**.
  - **Median recovery time (time to restore green): 72 minutes**, up **13% year-over-year**; CircleCI's benchmark target is **60 minutes**. Mid-sized companies (21-50 employees) average roughly **3 hours** to recover **[V]**.
  - **Throughput:** overall average **+59% YoY** increase in daily workflow runs, but this is bimodal — **top 5% of teams: +97%** growth, **median team: +4%**, **bottom quartile: ~0%**. Split by branch type for the median team: **feature-branch throughput +15%**, but **main-branch throughput -7%** — i.e., more work is happening in branches but less of it is landing **[V]**.
  - CircleCI's own interpretation, worth quoting directly: **"Writing code is no longer the constraint. Review, validation, integration, recovery"** are where the bottleneck now sits **[V]**.
- **2025 report** (published March 2025): analyzed **nearly 15 million workflows** **[V]** (report metadata via search results; the full numeric MTTR/success-rate table sits behind the PDF at `circleci.com/landing-pages/assets/2025-state-of-software-delivery-report.pdf`, which I could not fully extract this session — the one figure surfaced was an MTTR reference around **29 minutes**, but I was **unable to confirm** whether that is a median, mean, or a specific cohort's number, so mark this figure **[R]**/unverified and do not cite it without pulling the PDF directly).
- **Trend across 2024→2026:** every public summary agrees success rates and recovery times have been degrading release over release even as raw run-volume climbs — consistent with the "generation isn't the bottleneck anymore" thesis.

### 3. LinearB / PR cycle-time benchmarks

- **2025 Engineering Benchmarks Report:** **6.1M+ pull requests** across **3,000+ organizations**, 32 countries, published **Jan 8, 2025** **[V]** ([LinearB blog](https://linearb.io/blog/2025-engineering-benchmarks-insights)). Tiering methodology: **Elite = top 10%, Strong = top 30%, Fair = top 60%, Needs Focus = bottom 40%** by percentile **[V]**. Headline finding: **PR size is the single biggest driver of cycle time** — smaller PRs correlate strongly with faster review and merge **[V]**.
- **2026 Software Engineering Benchmarks Report:** **8.1M+ PRs** across **4,800 organizations**, 42 countries **[V]** ([LinearB, "AI PR Review Takes 5.3x Longer"](https://baeseokjae.github.io/posts/linearb-2026-engineering-benchmarks/); corroborated by [PR Lens](https://prlens.dev/guides/why-ai-pull-requests-wait-longer-for-review)).
  - **Elite cycle time (first commit → merge): under 25 hours at P75** (some summaries state "elite teams close the loop in under 26 hours" — treat 25-26h P75 as the elite band) **[V]**.
  - **Elite PR size:** figures vary by summarizing source — one cites **under ~194-219 lines**, another states elite teams "keep PRs under 100 lines to stay below the 25-hour cycle-time band" **[V]** (numbers are close but not perfectly reconciled across secondary write-ups of the same report; the underlying report is the authority).
  - **Elite review time: under 6 hours**; "Strong" tier: up to **13 hours** **[V]**.
  - **Elite pickup time (open → first review comment): under 7 hours** **[V]**.
  - **AI-assisted PR pickup penalty:** agentic AI PRs wait **5.3x longer** for reviewer pickup than unassisted PRs at P75 — **1,055 minutes vs. 201 minutes** **[V]**. A different secondary summary states the multiplier as **4.6x** — both figures appear in circulation citing the same 2026 report; likely reflect different percentile cuts or dataset slices. Use **5.3x @ P75, 1,055 vs 201 min** as the more precisely-sourced figure.
  - Once review _starts_, AI PRs are actually **faster to close**: ~**194 minutes** vs **252 minutes** for human-authored PRs **[V]**.
  - AI-assisted PRs are **2.6x larger** at P75 than unassisted PRs (**408 vs. 157 lines**), which is the leading explanatory variable for the pickup-time penalty **[V]**.
  - Broader 2026 AI-adoption findings (compiled across LinearB, and echoed by other trend pieces): teams using AI coding agents report **98% more PRs opened**, alongside a **91% increase in PR review time** and a **441% increase in median PR review time**; **31% of PRs now merge with zero review**; **bugs per developer up 54%** **[V]** (aggregated from [The Review Bottleneck, dev.to 2026](https://dev.to/code-board/the-review-bottleneck-why-more-ai-code-means-slower-teams-in-2026-1e5n) and [AgentConn "10x PRs, 1x Reviewers"](https://agentconn.com/blog/10x-prs-1x-reviewers-code-quality-bottleneck-gate-2026/), both of which cite LinearB/industry aggregate data — treat these compiled numbers as **[V]**-for-"reported-in-2026-press" but somewhat **[R]**-adjacent for exact methodology, since I did not reach the primary numeric table for every one of these specific figures).

### 4. Google / Meta code review research

- **Google, "Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google" (Sadowski et al., ICSE 2018 SEIP track):** median review latency for the _entire_ review process is **under 4 hours** across all change sizes; for small changes, median wait for first feedback is **under 1 hour**; for very large changes, roughly **5 hours** **[V]** ([paper PDF](https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf); [research.google listing](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/)). This is explicitly contrasted in follow-on literature with contemporaneous studies at other companies: median time-to-approval reported as **14.7-19.8 hours** across several Microsoft projects and **17.5 hours** at AMD **[V]** (as surfaced via search synthesis of the paper's related-work comparisons — worth re-verifying page numbers against the PDF directly before quoting in a formal doc).
- **Meta, "Using Nudges to Accelerate Code Reviews at Scale" (ESEC/FSE 2022):** Meta's 2020 baseline showed **85% of developers satisfied** with the code review process overall, but _less_ satisfied with review **speed**. Meta shipped **NudgeBot**, which pings reviewers on diffs stale ≥24h, targeting **p75 Time in Review**. A cluster-randomized A/B test on **30,000+ engineers** found statistically significant reductions: **time in review -6.8%**, **time to first reviewer action -9.9%** **[V]** ([Meta Engineering blog, "Move faster, wait less"](https://engineering.fb.com/2022/11/16/culture/meta-code-review-time-improving/); [ACM DL paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3540250.3549104)).
- **Small-PR effect:** both the LinearB benchmark data above and Google's own research agree — smaller diffs get picked up and reviewed dramatically faster; this is one of the most consistently replicated findings across every source consulted (Google 2018, Meta 2022, LinearB 2025/2026).

### 5. "10-minute build" and local feedback-loop targets

- **Origin:** the "ten-minute build" is an Extreme Programming (XP) guideline, popularized by Martin Fowler's "Continuous Integration" essay and Humble & Farley's _Continuous Delivery_ (Addison-Wesley, 2010). Fowler: _"For most projects... the XP guideline of a ten minute build is perfectly within reason... every minute you reduce off the build time is a minute saved for each developer every time they commit"_ **[V]** (paraphrase confirmed via search synthesis of martinfowler.com content; recommend re-fetching martinfowler.com/articles/continuousIntegration.html directly before quoting verbatim, since I did not fetch that exact page this session).
- **Pre-commit hook duration guidance (2025-2026 practitioner consensus, not a single canonical source):** a commonly cited "10-second rule" — hooks longer than ~10s get bypassed/hated by developers; several practitioner guides tighten this further, recommending **under 5 seconds** for a typical commit (staged-files-only lint/typecheck), with a hard ceiling around **15-20 seconds** before hooks are considered "bad" **[V]**-as-"commonly stated," but this is aggregate practitioner opinion (blogs, DEV.to posts, MLOps course), **not** a single authoritative research source — treat as **[R]**-leaning despite multiple independent write-ups converging on similar numbers. Consistent advice: full test suites belong in CI, not pre-commit; lint-staged-style "changed files only" scoping is the standard technique **[V]**.
- **DevEx-framework framing of the same idea (2023):** _"A CI pipeline that returns results in 90 seconds creates a fundamentally different experience than one that takes 25 minutes"_ — used illustratively in the DevEx literature (Noda/Forsgren/Storey/Greiler) to argue feedback-loop _speed_, not just correctness, is a first-class productivity lever **[V]** (paraphrase via secondary DevEx summaries; see Part 2 below for the primary paper).

### 6. Merge queue benchmarks and practices

- **GitHub's own merge queue config guidance:** "build concurrency" (max simultaneous `merge_group` webhook dispatches) is configurable **1-100**; "maximum group size" (PRs batched into one temporary branch) **defaults to 5** — GitHub's stated rationale is that 5 "strikes a good balance" between too many temporary branches (small batches) and higher failure likelihood from more PRs sharing one validation run (large batches); "merge limits" (min/max PRs merged together, plus a timeout for waiting on a minimum) are configurable **1-100** **[V]** ([GitHub Docs, "Managing a merge queue"](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/configuring-pull-request-merges/managing-a-merge-queue)).
- **Uber's SubmitQueue** (open-sourced, EuroSys'19 paper "Keeping master green at scale"): a **speculative** merge queue — rather than validating PRs strictly one-at-a-time, it speculatively rebases and validates multiple changes in parallel against _predicted future states_ of HEAD; on validation failure it isolates the offending change and retries the rest automatically, without human intervention **[V]** ([uber/submitqueue GitHub](https://github.com/uber/submitqueue); [Adrian Colyer's summary, "Keeping master green at scale"](https://blog.acolyer.org/2019/04/18/keeping-master-green-at-scale/)). This speculative-parallel-validation pattern is the conceptual ancestor of GitHub's, Mergify's, and Trunk's merge queues.
- **Mergify guidance:** the queue waits for pending checks with a configurable timeout (**minimum 60 seconds**), with an "auto" mode that computes the timeout from the **P95 of recent CI runtime** plus a safety margin; Mergify also runs speculative parallel validation of multiple queued PRs **[V]** ([Mergify docs, "Using Queue Rules"](https://docs.mergify.com/merge-queue/rules/)).
- **Trunk.io guidance:** distinguishing feature — detects a test that fails speculatively in the queue but _passes_ when re-tried further back, auto-classifies it as flaky, and keeps merging rather than stalling the whole queue **[V]** ([Trunk.io, "Merge Queue"](https://trunk.io/merge-queue)).
- **General practitioner rule of thumb, widely repeated in 2025-2026 vendor content:** _"if your CI takes 30 minutes, developers will wait hours for merges"_ — i.e., queue wait time scales with CI duration multiplied by queue depth, so CI speed is the primary lever for keeping queue time reasonable, batching/timeout tuning is secondary **[V]**-as-"commonly stated" (multiple vendor comparison pages independently make this point; treat the _causal logic_ as solid, the specific "hours" framing as **[R]**-level illustrative rather than measured).
- I did not find a single authoritative "typical time-in-queue" industry-wide number (e.g., "median PR spends X minutes in a merge queue") — this appears to be a **research gap**; the closest proxy is CircleCI's 72-minute median recovery time and LinearB's pickup-time figures above, but neither is queue-time specifically.

### 7. Flaky test rates

- **Google's published data** (via the Google Testing Blog and academic follow-ups): approximately **1.5% of test runs** at Google exhibit flaky behavior on any given execution; roughly **16% of all tests** show flakiness _at some point_; when a post-submit test transitions from passing to failing, **~84% of the time** it's a flaky result rather than a genuine regression **[V]** ([search synthesis of Google Testing Blog "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them"](https://daily.dev/posts/google-testing-blog-flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we-mitigate-them-4czmq00pe); cross-referenced against academic surveys on test flakiness, e.g. [arXiv:2203.00483](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.00483)). Framed differently: **more than 1 in 7 tests** written at Google will occasionally fail for reasons unrelated to the code or test change under review.
- **Industry target:** no single canonical "acceptable" flaky-rate number was found in this session's sources; the implicit standard from Google's own data (1.5% run-level flakiness) functions as an informal "this is what a mature, heavily-invested-in test system looks like" benchmark, not an explicitly stated target — treat the 1.5%/16%/84% figures as **[V]** (Google's reported data) but the notion of "1.5% is the target to aim for" as **[R]** (inference, not a stated goal).

### 8. AI code review tools — adoption and impact data

- **GitHub Copilot code review:** reached **general availability in April 2025** after surpassing **1 million developers** in preview; as of the most recent data point found, **60 million+** Copilot code reviews have been completed, with **12,000+ organizations** running it automatically on every PR **[V]** ([GitHub Blog, "60 million Copilot code reviews and counting"](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/60-million-copilot-code-reviews-and-counting/)). Review substance: **71%** of Copilot reviews surface actionable feedback (vs. **29%** with no comments), averaging **~5.1 comments per review** **[V]**. Broader Copilot adoption: **20 million cumulative users** (July 2025), generating an estimated **46%** of code written by users, adopted by **90% of Fortune 100** companies **[V]** (search-synthesized from GitHub/press sources — the 46%/90% figures are marketing-adjacent and should be treated as directional rather than independently audited).
- **Graphite Diamond / Graphite Agent** (AI code review, since renamed/absorbed into "Graphite Agent" as of **Oct 8, 2025**): reviewed **500,000+ pull requests** with a stated **<5% negative-comment rate** **[V]** ([Graphite blog / SuperbCrew coverage](https://www.superbcrew.com/diamond-sets-a-new-standard-for-ai-code-reviews-with-contextual-intelligence-and-speed/)). No independently-audited time-to-first-review figure was found for Graphite specifically in this session.
- **Factory.ai (Droid):** ships an automated-review workflow that skips draft PRs, fetches diff + existing comments, flags bugs/security/correctness issues with inline comments, and auto-approves when clean; runs a dedicated STRIDE-methodology security pass, deduplicated per PR **[V]** ([Factory docs, "Automated Code Review"](https://docs.factory.ai/cli/droid-exec/cookbook/code-review)). Factory's coordinator/specialist-droid architecture (separate code/review/docs/test/knowledge droids with explicit role boundaries) is directly relevant to a multi-agent-per-repo setup like DorkOS's.
- **2025-2026 empirical studies of agent-authored PRs on GitHub:**
  - A study of **567 GitHub PRs** generated by Claude Code across **157 open-source projects** found **83.8%** of agent-assisted PRs were eventually accepted/merged, with **54.9%** merged _without further modification_ **[V]** (search-synthesized from [arXiv listings on agentic PR studies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14745) — I was not able to fetch the paper's full text directly this session, so treat the exact percentages as **[V]**-for-"reported-in-search-synthesis" pending a direct PDF read).
  - A larger-scale study covering **33,000+ PRs** from **five major coding agents** and a separate analysis of **128,018 projects** estimated coding-agent adoption at **22.2%-28.7%** of projects as of **Feb 21, 2026** **[V]** (same arXiv cluster: "Agentic Much? Adoption of Coding Agents on GitHub," ACM TOSEM).
  - Agentic PRs for **documentation, CI, and build-config** tasks merge at **higher** rates than agentic PRs touching core logic **[V]**.

## Part 2 — Methodologies for managing and improving the pipeline

### 1. DORA metrics / Four Keys

- **What it is:** the four (now five, see above) canonical software-delivery metrics — deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, failed deployment recovery time, and (2024+) rework rate — derived from six years of DORA research, designed to be measured continuously and improved via targeted capability investment (DORA's "capabilities" catalog, e.g. trunk-based development, test automation, loosely-coupled architecture) **[V]**.
- **Four Keys (open source):** Google's `dora-team/fourkeys` project is a generalized ETL pipeline that ingests events from GitHub, GitLab, CircleCI, and Tekton (extensible to others) into BigQuery, with a Grafana/Data Studio dashboard showing daily systems data plus a rolling 90-day snapshot of all four metrics **[V]** ([GitHub: dora-team/fourkeys](https://github.com/dora-team/fourkeys); [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/using-the-four-keys-to-measure-your-devops-performance)).
- **How a tiny team applies it:** instrument GitHub Actions + the merge queue as the event source (deploy = merge to main / release tag; CFR = hotfix commits or reverted PRs within N days; recovery time = time between a failed required-check on main and the next green run). For a one-operator team, the point isn't dashboarding for a manager — it's giving an _agent_ a machine-readable trend line it can reference when deciding whether to slow down and add tests vs. keep shipping.

### 2. SPACE framework

- **What it is / who made it:** introduced 2021 by Nicole Forsgren, Margaret-Anne Storey, Chandra Maddila, Thomas Zimmermann, Brian Houck, and Jenna Butler, in "The SPACE of Developer Productivity" **[V]** ([Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-space-of-developer-productivity-theres-more-to-it-than-you-think/)). Five dimensions: **S**atisfaction & wellbeing, **P**erformance, **A**ctivity, **C**ommunication & collaboration, **E**fficiency & flow.
- **Core practice:** the framework's central claim is that no single metric (velocity, commits, lines of code) captures productivity — every team should pick at least one metric from **at least three** of the five dimensions, deliberately including at least one _perceptual_ (survey-based) measure alongside system-of-record metrics **[V]**.
- **Tiny-team application:** Activity/Efficiency data comes free from GitHub Actions + the merge queue; Satisfaction is the one dimension a one-operator team is at risk of skipping entirely because there's no separate "team" to survey — the DorkOS-relevant translation is asking whether the _operator_ (and, unusually, whether the _agents themselves_, given DorkOS's agent-etiquette framing) experience friction, not just whether CI is green.

### 3. DevEx framework (Noda, Storey, Forsgren, Greiler — 2023)

- **What it is:** formalized in a 2023 ACM Queue paper, distilling **25 sociotechnical factors** into **three dimensions**: **feedback loops** (speed/quality of response to developer actions — CI latency is the canonical example), **cognitive load** (mental burden of a task), and **flow state** (ability to sustain focused work) **[V]** ([summary via getdx.com / develocity.io](https://develocity.io/a-summary-devex-what-actually-drives-productivity-by-noda-et-al-2023/)).
- **Headline correlational finding:** each **1-point improvement** in a DevEx survey score correlates with **~13 minutes saved per developer per week** (~10 hours/year) **[V]** (widely cited figure from the DX/getdx.com ecosystem's own research; treat as **[V]**-for-"as reported by the framework's own commercial steward," i.e., corroborate independently before treating as a hard causal number).
- **Tiny-team application:** the framework explicitly names CI latency as a feedback-loop lever (their own example: 90-second vs. 25-minute CI creates "a fundamentally different experience"), which converts directly into a design target: keep the loop an agent waits on (lint/typecheck/targeted test) under a couple of minutes, and let anything longer run asynchronously behind the merge queue rather than blocking the agent's next action.

### 4. DX Core 4

- **What it is:** getdx.com's unifying framework layered on top of DORA, SPACE, and DevEx — four metrics: **Speed** (TrueThroughput®: PRs merged/week, complexity-weighted), **Effectiveness** (Developer Experience Index, DXI — a 14-item Likert survey aggregate covering code quality, focus time, CI/CD experience, etc.), **Quality** (Change Failure Rate), and **Impact** (% of engineering time on new capabilities vs. KTLO) **[V]** ([DX Core 4 guide](https://docs.getdx.com/dx-core-4/)).
- **Tiny-team application:** DXI is survey-based and designed for teams of people; for a single-operator/many-agent shop it doesn't map cleanly, but the _shape_ — pairing a hard throughput number with a hard quality number so speed gains can't be claimed without a stability check — is directly reusable even with n=1 human.

### 5. Value Stream Mapping / Flow Framework (Mik Kersten)

- **What it is:** introduced in Kersten's _Project to Product_ (2018); tracks four work types (features, defects, technical debt, risk) against five metrics — **Flow Velocity, Flow Time, Flow Efficiency, Flow Load, Flow Distribution** **[V]** ([flowframework.org](https://flowframework.org/ffc-discover/)).
- **Flow Efficiency benchmark (industry-wide, not CI-specific):** most teams measure **15-25% flow efficiency**, meaning **75-85% of a work item's total elapsed time is spent waiting**, not being actively worked **[V]** ([Allstacks / LinearB summaries of Flow Framework data](https://linearb.io/blog/5-key-flow-metrics)). This is the direct conceptual parallel to the CircleCI/LinearB PR-pickup-time data above — most delay is queueing, not doing.
- **Tiny-team application:** map a PR's life exactly like a Kanban card — coding time (active) vs. pickup + review + merge-queue wait (idle) — and treat any period where an agent-authored PR sits un-reviewed as "wait time" to be measured and attacked, exactly as LinearB's pickup-time data suggests is now the dominant cost.

### 6. Theory of Constraints (Goldratt)

- **What it is:** from Goldratt's 1984 book _The Goal_ — "a system can only move as fast as its slowest part"; improvement means finding the current bottleneck, subordinating everything else to it, elevating it, then finding the _next_ bottleneck (the constraint moves) **[V]** ([DZone, "Applying the Theory of Constraints to Software Development"](https://dzone.com/articles/apply-theory-constraints-software-development-bottlenecks); [Steve Smith, "Deployment pipeline design and the Theory of Constraints"](https://www.stevesmith.tech/blog/deployment-pipeline-design-and-the-theory-of-constraints/)).
- **Direct relevance to this exact moment:** the 2025-2026 industry data above **is** a live Theory-of-Constraints case study — the constraint used to be code generation (slow humans), agents elevated that constraint, and the constraint predictably moved to human/automated review and merge-queue capacity, exactly as TOC predicts it must. The practical takeaway for a tiny team: don't optimize CI speed or agent throughput further until you've confirmed _review_ (not CI runtime) is no longer the binding constraint — optimizing a non-constraint doesn't move the system's overall throughput.

### 7. Kaizen / PDCA / Toyota Kata / blameless postmortems

- **PDCA (Deming):** Plan-Do-Check-Act — a four-step experimental loop; Deming's variant explicitly advises **stopping to investigate deviations** rather than pushing through them **[V]** ([Wikipedia: PDCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDCA), cross-checked against methodsandtools.com).
- **Toyota Kata (Mike Rother):** a structured daily habit/routine for continuous improvement — the "Improvement Kata" (aim for a target condition, iterate toward it via PDCA-style experiments) plus the "Coaching Kata" (a mentor asks structured questions rather than prescribing solutions) **[V]** ([methodsandtools.com summary](https://www.methodsandtools.com/archive/toyotakata.php)).
- **Blameless postmortems:** treat most failures as arising from systemic/process gaps rather than individual negligence; structured around questions like "what conditions allowed this to happen" and "what safeguards were missing," with published action items, owners, and due dates, and a tracked **closure rate** to prevent repeat incidents **[V]** (search-synthesized from multiple postmortem-practice write-ups; this is now standard SRE/DevOps practice, popularized originally by Etsy and Google SRE).
- **Tiny-team application:** every CI/merge-queue failure an agent hits is a PDCA cycle in miniature — the postmortem doesn't need a meeting, it needs a durable artifact (a changelog fragment or ADR-style note) recording what broke, why, and what changed, so the same failure mode doesn't recur silently three PRs later.

### 8. SRE: SLOs for CI, error budgets, toil reduction

- **CI-specific SLO examples cited in current practitioner guidance:** "builds start running within 1 minute, error budget = 33 builds/4-week period exceeding that"; "commits are tested and the author notified within 5 minutes, error budget = 33 builds/4-week period exceeding that" **[V]** ([Buildkite, "Applying SRE principles to CI/CD"](https://buildkite.com/resources/blog/applying-sre-principles-to-cicd/)).
- **Error-budget mechanics:** once the budget is exhausted (0% remaining), standard SRE practice is to auto-block new feature deploys and redirect capacity to stability work — some CI/CD implementations wire this directly into the pipeline as a gate **[V]** (same source cluster).
- **Toil:** SRE's canonical definition of toil is repetitive, manual, automatable work; the classic SRE target is capping toil at roughly **50% of an SRE's time**, with the other half spent building automation that eliminates toil going forward **[V]** ([Google SRE Workbook, "Implementing SLOs"](https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/) and related SRE canon — the 50% figure is Google's original published guidance from the SRE book series, treated as well-established rather than freshly verified this session, so flagged **[R]** despite being extremely well known).
- **Tiny-team application:** an explicit CI SLO (e.g., "p90 required-check completion < 15 minutes," "p90 merge-queue time-in-queue < X minutes") gives an agent a concrete, machine-checkable threshold to alert on, rather than a vague "CI feels slow" complaint — which is exactly the gap a benchmarks report like this one is meant to close.

### 9. Platform engineering / Developer Productivity Engineering (DPE) and build observability

- **DPE** is Gradle's term for treating build/test/CI performance as a first-class engineering discipline with its own tooling and metrics, rather than an afterthought of "DevOps."
- **OpenTelemetry CI/CD semantic conventions:** a dedicated CI/CD Observability Special Interest Group within OpenTelemetry has defined semantic conventions (spans, metrics, logs) specifically for pipeline execution, explicitly to solve the fragmentation problem where every CI vendor reports status in its own incompatible format **[V]** ([opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/cicd/](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/semconv/cicd/); [CNCF blog, Nov 2024](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2024/11/04/opentelemetry-is-expanding-into-ci-cd-observability/)).
- **CircleCI now supports OTLP export** to Honeycomb, Datadog, and Grafana natively, meaning CI telemetry can flow into general-purpose observability platforms rather than living only in vendor-specific dashboards **[V]** ([CircleCI changelog](https://circleci.com/changelog/opentelemetry-support-for-ci-cd)).
- **Tiny-team application:** even without adopting a commercial CI-observability product, structuring internal logging/metrics around the OTel CI/CD semantic convention names (`cicd.pipeline.*`, etc.) keeps the door open to plugging into Honeycomb/Datadog later without a rewrite, and gives any dashboard-building agent a standard vocabulary instead of an ad hoc one.

### 10. Engineering decision tracking: ADRs and CI-changelog / experiment tracking

- **ADRs** are the standard mechanism (already in use in this repo) for durably recording _why_ a CI/merge-queue configuration choice was made, which matters more than usual here because merge-queue and SLO tuning decisions are exactly the kind of thing that looks arbitrary in isolation but was actually a response to a specific measured problem.
- **"CI changelog" / experiment-style tracking:** the PDCA/SRE pattern above (hypothesis + metric + review date) is the same shape as a lightweight experiment log — practitioner guidance converges on recording, for every CI/pipeline change: what was slow, the hypothesis for why, the specific metric expected to move, and a date to check whether it did. This is not a single named industry framework so much as the common intersection of PDCA, SRE error-budget policy, and Kaizen practice applied specifically to pipeline configuration.

### 11. Test impact analysis / selective testing / shift-left / trunk-based development

- **Trunk-based development** is a DORA-identified capability: merge to a single shared branch (main) early and often; the pairing of TBD with a fast automated test suite that validates every commit to trunk **is** DORA's operational definition of continuous integration **[V]** ([DORA capabilities: trunk-based development](https://dora.dev/capabilities/trunk-based-development/)).
- **Test impact analysis / selective testing (2025-2026 practice):** run only the subset of tests whose dependency graph touches the changed files, rather than the full suite on every PR; current guidance explicitly frames this as AI-assisted (agents determine which tests actually correlate with a given diff) **[V]** (search-synthesized from multiple 2025-2026 shift-left/CI-optimization write-ups).
- **Shift-left target:** current practitioner guidance recommends keeping the **commit-level feedback loop under 10 minutes** via parallelization, caching, and selective test execution **[V]** — directly consistent with the XP "10-minute build" guideline from three decades earlier and the DevEx framework's feedback-loop emphasis.

## Part 3 — Multi-agent / AI-agent-heavy development: how review, CI load, and merge queues change

This is the fastest-moving and most directly relevant part of the research. Every source from late 2025 through mid-2026 describes the same structural shift, from different vantage points:

- **Stripe ("Minions"):** homegrown one-shot coding agents now merge **over 1,000 PRs per week**, with the explicit design thesis that constrained, one-shot agent "blueprints" (deterministic orchestration nodes alternating with bounded open-ended agent loops) outperform long conversational agent loops for well-defined tasks on both latency and cost **[V]** ([Stripe Dot Dev blog, "Minions: Stripe's one-shot, end-to-end coding agents"](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents); [ByteByteGo summary, "How Stripe's Minions Ship 1,300 PRs a Week"](https://blog.bytebytego.com/p/how-stripes-minions-ship-1300-prs)). Workflow: an engineer messages a Slack bot, walks away, and returns to a PR that has already passed automated tests and is staged for human review only. Separately, Stripe reports agent traffic to its developer docs went from **<5% to ~40%** of total docs traffic across 2025 **[V]**.
- **Shopify:** CTO Mikhail Parakhin is on record that AI tool adoption "shot from gradual to nearly vertical in December 2025," and that despite this, he is fixated specifically on **PR review** as the unsolved problem — "the real bottleneck in AI coding is no longer generation, but review, CI/CD, and deployment stability" **[V]** ([dev.to summary of Parakhin's public commentary](https://dev.to/o96a/the-real-bottleneck-in-ai-coding-isnt-generation-its-everything-else-21l3); [Latent Space interview](https://www.latent.space/p/shopify)). Shopify's own reported paradox: **AI-written code has fewer bugs per line than human-written code, but because so much more of it ships, the absolute count of bugs reaching production has increased** **[V]**. Shopify's two 2026 mitigation strategies: (1) parallel agents — ~10 agents working simultaneously, all still requiring human review/merge; (2) sequential critique loops — deep-reasoning models interrogating their own output for **45+ minute** self-review sessions before a human ever sees the diff **[V]**.
- **Factory.ai:** architected explicitly around a coordinator agent that decomposes work and dispatches to role-scoped specialist droids (code / review / docs / test / knowledge) rather than one generalist agent doing everything — a direct structural response to the same review-bottleneck problem, by making review itself an explicit, dedicated agent role rather than a human-only gate **[V]** (see Part 1 §8 for sourcing).
- **GitHub's own data (Copilot code review):** 12,000+ orgs running automated review on every PR, 60M+ reviews completed, 71% surfacing actionable feedback — read together with the GitHub-adjacent academic studies (83.8% agent-PR merge rate; 22-29% of active GitHub projects now show agent-adoption signals as of Feb 2026), the picture is that **AI review of AI-authored code is becoming the default first pass**, with humans increasingly reviewing the AI reviewer's output rather than the raw diff **[V]** (synthesized across Part 1 §8 sources).
- **The 2025→2026 numeric core of "review is now the bottleneck":**
  - LinearB 2026: AI-assisted PRs wait **5.3x longer** for pickup (1,055 vs 201 min @ P75), driven by being **2.6x larger** (408 vs 157 lines @ P75) **[V]**.
  - Aggregate 2026 press/vendor data: **98% more PRs opened**, **91% increase in review time**, **441% increase in median review time**, **31% of PRs merging with zero review**, **54% more bugs per developer** among teams with high AI-agent adoption **[V]** (see Part 1 §3 sourcing note on methodology confidence).
  - CircleCI 2026: despite **+59%** average workflow-run growth, **main-branch throughput for the median team actually fell 7%** and **success rate hit a 5-year low (70.8%)** — i.e., more attempts, not more landed changes **[V]**.
  - DORA 2025: AI adoption is now positively correlated with throughput (reversing the 2024 finding) but remains negatively correlated with delivery **stability** — DORA's explicit warning is that teams without strong automated testing, strong version control discipline, and fast feedback loops will see this instability gap widen, not close, as agent volume grows **[V]**.
- **Convergent prescription across every source above** (Stripe, Shopify, Factory, DORA, LinearB, CircleCI): the fix is not slowing agents down, it's (a) keeping PRs small even when agent-authored (the single strongest lever in every dataset), (b) inserting an automated review pass _before_ the PR reaches a human — whether a dedicated review agent (Factory's model), a bot (GitHub Copilot review, Graphite Agent), or a structured self-critique loop (Shopify's 45-minute sequential critique) — so humans review a pre-screened diff rather than raw agent output, and (c) treating merge-queue and CI capacity as the newly-binding constraint per Theory of Constraints, meaning further agent-speed optimization is close to pointless until review/merge throughput is addressed first.

## Research Gaps & Limitations

- I could not extract the literal numeric tables (deployment frequency / lead time / CFR / recovery time by team archetype) from the primary 2025 DORA report PDF — the crawlable pages are narrative summaries; pulling the PDF directly from `dora.dev` is recommended before citing exact 2025 archetype-level thresholds in a decision document.
- The CircleCI **2025** report's exact median workflow duration and success-rate figures were not fully extracted (only the 2026 report's numbers were confirmed with confidence); the one 2025 MTTR figure surfaced (~29 min) could not be confirmed as median vs. mean and should be treated as unverified.
- Two different multipliers for "AI PR pickup-time penalty" are circulating for what appears to be the same underlying 2026 LinearB dataset (4.6x and 5.3x) — likely different percentile cuts, not confirmed which is authoritative.
- No single authoritative "typical time spent in a GitHub/Mergify/Trunk merge queue" industry-wide figure was found; this remains inferred from adjacent metrics (CI duration, recovery time, pickup time) rather than measured directly.
- The "acceptable" flaky-test-rate _target_ (as opposed to Google's _observed_ rate) is not something any source stated explicitly as a goal; only Google's own observed 1.5%/16%/84% figures were found.
- Pre-commit-hook duration guidance (5s / 10s / 20s figures) reflects converging practitioner opinion across multiple blogs, not a single controlled study — treat as directional.

## Contradictions & Disputes

- **DORA 2024 vs. 2025 on AI and throughput:** the 2024 DORA report found AI adoption correlated with _reduced_ throughput; the 2025 report reverses this and finds a _positive_ throughput correlation, while the _negative_ stability correlation persists in both years. This is a genuine year-over-year reversal in DORA's own findings, not a citation error — worth flagging explicitly in any summary that references "the DORA AI finding," since which year's finding is being invoked matters.
- **LinearB pickup-time multiplier (4.6x vs. 5.3x)** — see Research Gaps above.
- **CircleCI's framing ("review/validation/integration/recovery is the bottleneck") vs. LinearB's framing ("PR size is what drives pickup-time penalty")** are not contradictory but emphasize different levers — CircleCI's data is about the _validation pipeline_, LinearB's is about _human reviewer_ behavior. Both are true simultaneously and reinforce the same Theory-of-Constraints reading: the constraint moved past generation into multiple downstream stages at once, not just one.
